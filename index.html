Heritage on the high street

Chapter 01 — Spatial Layouts & Micro-Geography

Where Does Culture Cluster?

Melbourne's inner suburbs are not a culinary monoculture. The CBD's dense Chinese and Japanese concentration, Carlton's iconic Italian corridor along Lygon Street, and the Vietnamese pockets of North Melbourne each reflect layers of migration history, community formation, and commercial identity. The two charts below map this geography at two different scales venue by venue, and cuisine by cuisine.

Interactive Map

Melbourne Dining Venues by Cuisine Type, 2024

Each circle represents a single venue. Circle size encodes seat capacity, larger venues cast a bigger footprint. Colour encodes cuisine type. Use the dropdown to isolate individual cuisines and trace their geographic patterns. Hover over any venue for full details.

Capacity Distribution

Seating Capacity Profile by Cuisine

This boxplot exposes the architectural scale of each cuisine type. Western/Pub and Chinese venues show the widest range, from intimate 10-seat eateries to 300-plus seat banquet halls while cafés maintain a tighter, more consistent footprint.

Scale Comparison

Venues vs. Total Seat Capacity by Suburb

Southbank has far fewer venues than Carlton but comparable seat capacity, its restaurants are simply larger, serving theatre-goers and arts precinct visitors who arrive in groups.

Chapter 02 — Longitudinal Market Shifts, 2018–2024

Seven Years of Culinary Evolution

The 2020 pandemic left a visible scar across Melbourne's dining landscape, but recovery was uneven. Some cuisines bounced back with renewed intensity; others contracted permanently. The streamgraph captures this as a single flowing shape, while the line chart and bump chart reveal which suburbs and cuisines led the charge back to the table.

Streamgraph

The Flow of Melbourne's Culinary Scene, 2018–2024

The streamgraph's centred stack means the overall shape is symmetric, allowing relative growth or contraction to be read off each layer's width. The narrow 2020 waist across all layers captures the pandemic's sudden, total disruption followed by the broad re-expansion that followed.

Regional Trends

Establishment Growth by Suburb, 2018–2024

Hover over any line to highlight a suburb's trajectory. The CBD dominates in absolute terms, but note Carlton and Southbank's rapid post-2022 recovery, which rebuilt faster than peripheral residential suburbs, reflecting their reliance on tourism and foot traffic as much as local residents.

Bump Chart

Cuisine Popularity Rankings, 2018–2024

Korean cuisine climbed the rankings consistently across all seven years. Western / Pub formats slid, reflecting a cultural shift away from traditional pub dining toward more diverse casual formats a change visible across all Melbourne suburbs.

Chapter 03 — Dual-Source Demographic Correlations

Does Heritage Shape What We Eat?

Does a suburb's cultural makeup predict which restaurants thrive there? Carlton's Italian heritage clearly supports its restaurant density, the CBD's large Chinese-ancestry population coincides with its cuisine dominance; yet some cultures appear to under-index commercially, suggesting gaps in representation.

Correlation Scatter

Italian Ancestry vs. Italian Restaurant Count, 2021

Each point is one of five inner suburbs, positioned by Italian ancestry share (x) and Italian restaurant count (y). Carlton — with over 10% of residents claiming Italian ancestry — also has the highest concentration of Italian establishments. .

Cultural Matrix Heatmap

Suburb × Cuisine Concentration, 2021

The CBD's dominance spans every category, while Carlton's distinctive Italian column confirms its heritage concentration. The heatmap also reveals which cuisine–suburb combinations are completely absent.

Dumbbell Plot

Where Cultures Over- & Under-Index in Melbourne's Dining Scene

For four major cultural groups, each row compares a suburb's population ancestry share (hollow circle) with that cuisine's share of total restaurants (solid circle). A solid circle to the right of the hollow circle means the cuisine is commercially over-represented relative to the local population. A solid circle to the left means it lags behind population share — suggesting either lower commercial density or higher dining-out rates for that community.

Chapter 04 — Seating Layout & Infrastructure Capacity

The Spaces We Share

The pandemic permanently rewired how Melbourne restaurants deploy their physical space. Outdoor seating — once a nice-to-have for warm summer evenings — became existential during lockdowns, and council policy moved to accommodate it. The charts below trace the remarkable shift toward alfresco dining, showing which suburbs and cuisine types have most aggressively embraced open-air infrastructure and what the city-wide split looks like in 2024.

Area Chart

Indoor vs. Outdoor Seat Growth, 2018–2024

The layered areas track total seat capacity split by seating type.

Donut Chart

City-Wide Seating Mix, 2024

Nearly one in four seats across Melbourne's inner-city dining scene is now outdoors, a remarkable shift from pre-pandemic norms when outdoor capacity was a fraction of this.

100% Stacked Bar

Indoor vs. Outdoor Seating Mix by Suburb, 2024

The normalised bars show each suburb's proportional seating split. Southbank and East Melbourne,adjacent to the Yarra's riverfront promenades and Royal Botanic Gardens, hold the highest outdoor ratios. Kensington and Parkville remain almost entirely indoor, reflecting their residential character and limited commercial streetscape for alfresco tables.
